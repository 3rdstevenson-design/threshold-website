Most rehab ends in the wrong place. You get cleared when the pain settles and you can handle daily life, and then you're handed a date to return to your sport. Four weeks. Six weeks. Twelve. The number was decided by a protocol, not by you.

That's the gap I built Threshold to close. Less pain is the start of the conversation, and your sport is somewhere far past it. The distance between those two points is real, and almost nobody coaches you across it.

<ThresholdContinuum />

## Less pain and sport-ready are two different addresses

Standard physical therapy is built to get you to a functional baseline. Walk without a limp, climb stairs, do a bodyweight squat that no longer flares. For a lot of people that's the whole goal, and it's a fine one.

It isn't your goal. You want to cut at full speed, plant and trust the leg, be first off the line, serve without guarding the shoulder. The clinic stops measuring you long before that point, so you get discharged at the baseline and left to figure out the rest on your own.

That space between discharge and real sport demand is where I see athletes and patients get stuck. The knee feels fine in the kitchen and falls apart on the field. The shoulder is quiet at rest and cranky an hour into the match. The body part isn't lying to you. It's telling you the bridge was never built.

## The calendar is the wrong way to decide

Here's the part that does real damage. When return is decided by time since surgery instead of by what you can actually do, you get sent back unprepared. The research has been moving away from time-based clearance for years, toward criterion-based decisions: objective strength, hop and jump testing, change of direction, and honest psychological readiness.

Roughly half of return-to-sport studies still lean on time alone. That's the old way, and athletes pay for it with reinjuries that are usually worse than the first one.

I work the other way. Each stage opens when you've earned it, and time is one input among several, not the whole decision.

## What the crossing actually looks like

The bridge is a sequence, and you always know where you are on it. I restore strength and control, then build the ability to absorb and produce force, then layer in change of direction, then sport speed and the chaos of a real game.

Each rung earns the next. You feel progress early because hands-on treatment starts in session one, and the load climbs on purpose rather than on a calendar. The CROSS method maps onto this whole crossing: clinical assessment finds where the system failed, reconditioning starts the work, ownership rebuilds the trust an injury takes from you, the sequence carries you across, and I stay with you the entire way.

## Why I do it this way

I started my apprenticeship under an Olympic performance coach at 20, before PT school. I learned sprint mechanics and the weight room from the inside, then brought that into my doctorate and my work with sprinters prepping for the Tokyo and Paris Olympic Games.

That combination is rare, and it's the reason I see the gap so clearly. A rehab background alone tends to stop at the function necessary for daily living. A performance background alone tends to skip the tissue that's still healing. A proper crossing from rehab to performance needs both.
