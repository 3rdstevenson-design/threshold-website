The day you get discharged feels like the finish line. Someone tells you you're cleared, the visits stop, and you assume that means you're ready to play.

For an athlete, that's the edge of a cliff. Cleared means you've reached a baseline. Ready means you can meet the demands of your sport at full speed and trust your body to do it. Those are two very different places, and there's a gap between them that most plans leave you to cross alone.

<DischargeCliff />

## What cleared actually measures

Clearance usually means the boring, important stuff is back. The pain has backed off, you've got your range of motion, you can handle daily life. That's a real milestone and you should be glad to hit it.

It just doesn't say anything about cutting, sprinting, landing from a jump, or absorbing contact. And the pain backing off is not the same as it being gone. A shoulder that's okay doing the dishes still barks on an overhead serve. A knee that handles the stairs still nags when you try to decelerate at game speed. The tests that clear you and the demands that define your sport aren't the same tests.

## Why the cliff hurts people

When you treat clearance as readiness, you take the leap. Your body simply isn't built to withstand the demands your sport requires yet.

That's where the reinjury lives. It's also where the slow, nagging setbacks come from, the ones that never fully resolve because you keep loading a system that was only ever brought back to baseline. Manage that long enough and the limitation stops feeling temporary. It starts to feel like your ceiling.

## Building the bridge instead

The crossing is a sequence, and it's built on what you can do, not on the date you were discharged. I start loading early, then progress you through strength, power, change of direction, and sport speed, opening each stage only when you've earned it.

You always know where you are and where you're going next. That clarity is most of what's missing from the standard discharge, where the plan simply ends.
