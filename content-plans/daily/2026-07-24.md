## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** Your knee can be strong enough to play while your brain still is not, and that gap is where reinjuries happen.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, here's something I think about every single time I clear an athlete to go back after an ACL. We test strength, we test hop symmetry, we test change of direction, and when the numbers look good we send them back out. A study just came out on 822 patients after ACL reconstruction, and they checked three kinds of readiness at once: strength, performance, and psychological. All three of those lined up for less than a third of the athletes.

So most of the time the knee passes but the head does not, or the head is ready and the knee still lags behind. Think about what that means on the field. An athlete who tests strong but is scared to plant and cut is going to move differently, and that hesitation is where a lot of second injuries come from.

So how do you close that gap? You measure the fear directly. There's a questionnaire called the ACL-RSI, and a score of 65 or higher is the marker that someone is psychologically ready. I want that number climbing right alongside the strength number before anybody plays a real game.

So if you or your kid is coming back from a knee, ask two questions. Is the leg strong and symmetrical, and do you actually trust it under pressure. Both have to be yes.

Does that make sense? Clear the whole athlete, not just the joint.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** You cannot run your way to speed, and the research is pretty blunt about it.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. Every August I watch high school teams run gassers and hit the track and call it speed work, and then everybody wonders why nobody actually got faster. Conditioning and speed are two different qualities. Conditioning gets you through the fourth quarter. Speed is its own thing, and you build it in the weight room.

There's a new network meta-analysis on football players that ranked every training method head to head. For the 30 meter sprint, strength training came out on top, with an 88.8 percent probability of being the best option. The barbell beat the running programs.

Same story for jumping. Strength training had a 94 percent probability of being the best method for vertical jump. If you want an explosive athlete, you make them strong first, and the speed tends to follow.

So conditioning still has a job. It builds the engine so they hold up late in the game. If the goal is a faster, more explosive athlete though, the barbell comes first, and the running supports it.

Does that make sense? Stop trying to run kids into speed. Get them strong.
