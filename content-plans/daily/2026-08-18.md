## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** 248 professional soccer players trained for 8 weeks, and their first 10 yards got faster by half a percent.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
248 professional soccer players trained for 8 weeks, and their first 10 yards got faster by half a percent.

Half a percent. That's a rounding error.

The same athletes over 20 meters improved almost twice as much. 75% more gain at the longer distance than at the short one.

Okay, so what's going on there. The first 10 yards is mostly a strength and a body position problem, and these guys were already trained, already strong, already good at putting force into the ground behind them, so there was very little left on the table. Top speed is a different skill. It's how hard you hit the ground and how fast you get off it, and that one still had room to move.

Here's what I'd do with it. If your offseason is 8 weeks of 10 yard starts and sled pushes and nothing else, you're hammering the part of the sprint that's already close to maxed out. Add real top speed work. Flying runs, 20 to 40 yards, full effort, long rest, low volume. The researchers said it about as plainly as you can: low volume, high velocity.

And be honest about the timeline with your athletes. Sprint speed moves slowly in a trained kid, so a good 8 week block buys you a small number, and that small number is still the difference between getting there and getting there second.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** 87 studies just got ranked head to head, and after a lifting session, doing nothing beat every cold plunge protocol they tested.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
87 studies just got ranked head to head, and after a lifting session, doing nothing beat every cold plunge protocol they tested.

2,313 athletes. For strength, passive recovery came out on top.

Now hold that loosely, because the certainty on a lot of these comparisons is low and 85% of the subjects were male, so the evidence is thin in places. The direction has been the same for years though. You lift, you create inflammation, and that inflammation is the signal that tells the muscle to adapt. Sit in 50 degree water for 15 minutes right after and you blunt the signal you just paid for.

The plunge still earns a spot. That same review found moderate temperature, 10 to 15 minutes, helped muscle damage markers and helped how recovered athletes actually felt.

So put it where it pays. Tournament weekend, three lacrosse games in two days. Friday night game, Saturday film and lift. Sore, beat up, and you have to go again tomorrow. That's when you get in the tub.

Monday in the weight room chasing strength, skip it. Walk, eat, sleep. Your body handles the rest, and the soreness you're trying to erase is part of the reason the lift worked.

**Caption (copy-paste ready):**
A network meta-analysis just ranked cold water immersion protocols against each other and against doing nothing. 87 studies, 2,313 athletes.

For resistance training strength, passive recovery ranked highest. The plunge protocols came in behind it.

The certainty is low across a lot of those comparisons and 85% of the subjects were male, so hold it loosely. The mechanism has been pointing this way for a while: the inflammation you create in a lift is the signal that tells the muscle to adapt, and cold blunts it.

The plunge still has a job. That same review found moderate temperature immersion for 10 to 15 minutes helped muscle damage markers and perceived recovery.

That makes it a turnaround tool. Tournament weekend, back to back games, three lacrosse games in two days and you have to move again tomorrow. Get in the tub.

Monday in the weight room chasing strength, skip it and go for a walk instead.

Yu et al., BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation, 2026.
