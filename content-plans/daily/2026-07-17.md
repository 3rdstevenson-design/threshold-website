## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel

**Hook:** Before a hamstring tears, the GPS usually shows an athlete who was running less.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, I want to talk about the two games before a hamstring goes.

Researchers tracked 37 hamstring strains in professional LaLiga players, matched every injury against a control, and pulled the GPS data from the matches leading in. The players who got hurt had lower match exposure in the 1 and 2 games before the injury, and that reduced exposure explained a real chunk of the risk.

The sharpest single predictor was the game right before. Under about 328 meters of high-speed running. Under 64 minutes played.

Here's the mechanism, and I'm hedging a little, because this is professional men's soccer and most of my athletes are in high school. Your hamstring is built by sprinting. Fast running is the thing that keeps it tolerant to fast running. So when an athlete gets rotated out, or rides the bench a couple weeks, or sits 10 days with a tweak, that tissue quietly loses its exposure. Then Friday night comes, they chase a ball down the sideline, and they hit top speed cold.

A quiet week is a training problem.

So when one of my guys misses time, I get high-speed exposure back in before he plays. 2 or 3 build-ups to near top speed, twice that week, kind of a maintenance dose. It's small and it's boring and it protects the tissue.

Sprinting is the prevention.

Does that make sense?

## Draft B — Hot take Reel

**Hook:** Your foam roller is a warm-up tool, and you've been using it as a recovery plan.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, hot take. The foam roller earns its 5 minutes in the warm-up, and it's doing almost nothing for you after practice.

There's a meta-analysis in Frontiers in Physiology that pooled the foam rolling literature. Rolling before you train gave a small bump in sprint performance, about 0.7%, and a small bump in flexibility, about 4%. Effects on jump and strength were basically nothing.

Rolling after training slightly softened the drop in sprint and strength, and it cut soreness perception by about 6%. So it feels better, and I think that's real, and I'm fine with an athlete doing it for that reason. Feeling better has value.

But 6% less soreness is a nice feeling, and it's a long way from a recovery program. The authors' own conclusion puts foam rolling in the warm-up bucket and pulls it out of the recovery bucket.

Here's what actually recovers you. Sleep. Food. Lighter load. That's it. Those three are boring and free and they work, and there's no $40 piece of foam attached to any of them.

So keep the roller. Use it for 5 minutes before practice to get moving. Just don't spend 20 minutes on the floor after a game chasing 6% while you're sleeping 6 hours and skipping breakfast.

I'd rather you sleep 9.
