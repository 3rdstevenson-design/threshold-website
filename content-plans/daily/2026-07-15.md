## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** If your athlete is dragging by the third quarter, the problem might be that they are eating like a couch potato and training like a pro.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, here is something I see all the time with young athletes. They train two, three hours a day, they have practice and lifting and a game on the weekend, and they are eating like a kid who sits on the couch all day. There is a study on adolescent soccer players that found they were taking in about 1,929 calories a day while burning around 3,568. That is a deficit of over 1,600 calories, every single day.

Your body notices. When you are that far under what you are spending, it starts pulling back on the things that keep you on the field. Bone building slows down, and that is a big deal, because these are the exact years your skeleton is still laying down density, and that is when stress fractures start showing up. Recovery gets worse, you stay sore longer, and your speed and power fade late in games because there is nothing left in the tank.

The fix is simple. Eat enough to cover the work. For most of my athletes that means real meals plus snacks around training, and hitting protein through the day, somewhere around 1.5 grams per kilo of bodyweight, spread across the day instead of one giant dinner.

Fueling is training. If you are going to put in the work, feed the work.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** If your warm-up is sitting on the grass holding a hamstring stretch for 30 seconds, you are warming up to be slower.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. The old-school warm-up, where the coach lines everybody up and has them sit and hold long static stretches before a game, is making your athletes slower.

There is a study on elite soccer players that compared dynamic stretching to static stretching and a combination of the two right before repeated sprints. Dynamic stretching won across the board. The static stretching group did not sprint as well.

Here is the why. When you hold a muscle in a long stretch and then ask it to fire, it is kind of like pulling a rubber band out and leaving it loose for a while, the snap is gone for a bit. The research on this shows long holds, over about 60 seconds per muscle, can drop power and sprint speed by 4 to 7 percent. That is the difference between getting to the ball and watching it go by.

Stretching has its place. Save the long holds for after training, or on your own time. Before you compete, move. Leg swings, walking lunges, skips, a few build-up sprints, get the tissue warm and the nervous system firing.

Warm up to play fast.

Be Good.

**Caption (copy-paste ready):**
The line-everybody-up-and-hold-a-stretch warm-up is costing your athletes speed. A study on elite soccer players compared dynamic stretching to static stretching and a mix of the two before repeated sprints, and dynamic stretching won on every measure.

The mechanism is simple. Hold a muscle in a long static stretch, then ask it to produce force, and the output drops for a while. The meta-analysis on this puts long holds, over about 60 seconds per muscle, at a 4 to 7 percent hit to power and sprint speed. On the field that is the half step you needed.

Stretching still earns its place, just later in the day. Do the long holds after training. Before you compete, move: leg swings, walking lunges, skips, and a few build-up sprints to get warm and firing.

It's time to cross your threshold.
