## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** If your hamstrings keep grabbing, you're probably not sprinting enough.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, here's the one that surprises a lot of coaches. Coaches pull their athletes off sprinting to keep the hamstrings safe, then hammer Nordic curls and call it prevention.

A pilot study in Cureus put EMG sensors on field athletes and measured hamstring activity across the big prevention exercises. Sprinting lit the hamstrings up the most, more than the Nordic curl, and way more than the isokinetic machine work.

That tracks with what the hamstring actually does. At top speed it's catching the leg as it swings forward, eccentric, at a long length, under huge load. That's the exact moment hamstrings tear, so that's the exact moment you have to train.

Nordics are great, I still program them. But a Nordic is a slow drill, and a sprint is the real thing at game speed.

So here's Monday. Build sprinting in on purpose, a little at a time. Start with some buildups at 70 to 80 percent, then earn your way up to a couple of true max velocity runs each week, full recovery between them. The goal here is exposing the tissue to speed so it's ready when the game asks for it.

Treat sprinting like a skill you train and progress.

Hamstring that keeps barking on you? The fix might be the thing you've been avoiding.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Stop arguing about box jumps versus broad jumps. Your athletes don't care.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
I used to tell athletes the direction of the jump had to match the direction they sprint. Horizontal force for speed, so only broad jumps and bounds, box jumps are a waste. I hear coaches say it constantly.

A study in Scientific Reports actually tested it. They took semi-pro soccer players, split them into a horizontal jump group and a vertical jump group, 13 weeks, one plyo session a week. Then they measured sprint and change of direction.

Both groups got faster. Both improved their change of direction. And between the two groups, there was no meaningful difference.

What matters is that they jumped, with intent, consistently, and progressed the load over time.

Here's what I'd actually obsess over. Are they landing clean and quiet? Are they fully recovered so each rep is explosive instead of mushy? Are you adding a little every few weeks? That's where the speed comes from.

Pick the jump you can coach well and your athlete can do safely, then go be relentless about quality. The argument about horizontal versus vertical is mostly noise.

**Caption (copy-paste ready):**
Hot take: the horizontal versus vertical jump debate is mostly noise.

Coaches love to say speed is horizontal force, so box jumps are useless and you should only bound and broad jump. A Scientific Reports trial put it to the test: semi-pro soccer players, 13 weeks, one plyometric session a week, one group horizontal and one vertical. Both groups improved sprint and change of direction, and there was no meaningful difference between them.

What moves the needle is intent, clean landings, full recovery between reps, and progressive load over time. Pick the jump you can coach well and the athlete can do safely, then chase quality every single rep.

Works for football, lacrosse, basketball, any field or court athlete.
