## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel

**Hook:** If your athlete hasn't touched top speed since June, their hamstrings are the problem.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, the hamstring conversation always lands on Nordic curls, and Nordics are great, and I program them. Sprinting is the bigger lever.

There's a narrative review in the journal Sports where the whole argument sits right in the title. If you want to prevent hamstring injuries, run fast.

Here's the mechanism. When you go from 80 percent of your top speed up to 100 percent, the long head of the biceps femoris fires 67 percent harder through the terminal swing phase, that split second before the foot hits the ground. The other two hamstrings only climb 37 percent.

So the muscle that tears the most is the one that only gets fully loaded at true top speed.

Which means if your athlete never gets above 95 percent of their max in practice, that tissue never sees the demand, and then week one of the season asks for it anyway, at full speed, with a defender closing.

The programming piece from that review: 35 to 40 percent of sprint volume above 85 percent of max speed, and 15 to 20 percent above 95 percent. Introduce it progressively from preseason so you avoid spikes.

And a separate study on elite football turnarounds found that when the near-max exposure landed 2 days before the game, there were zero match hamstring injuries.

Go fast on purpose.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel

**Hook:** The most underrated supplement for your high school athlete is lunch.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, hot take. I listened to a Flex Diet episode with Dr. Emily Werner, she's the team dietitian for the 76ers, and the line that stuck with me was pro athletes chasing 200,000 dollar wellness gadgets while the basics sit there untouched.

Same thing happens at the high school level, it's just cheaper. Creatine, a pre-workout, two recovery powders, and a kid who ate a granola bar at 7am and nothing else until 8pm after practice.

Here's what that actually costs you. There's a narrative review on low energy availability, and when energy availability drops to 25 calories per kilo of fat-free mass per day, resting metabolic rate falls and T3, that's a thyroid hormone, drops right along with it.

That's the body turning off the lights to save power. Growth, repair, and adaptation all get dimmed.

One study inside that review had 55 percent of athletes screening positive for low energy availability, and in male cyclists who had it, 44 percent showed lumbar spine bone density Z-scores below negative one. That happens quietly, and you tend to find out mid-season.

So fix the floor first. Breakfast, a real lunch, food inside an hour after training, and something before bed.

The supplements are fine, they just work on top of an athlete who's actually fed.

Be Good.
