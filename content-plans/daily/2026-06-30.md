## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** If you take 3 weeks off this summer, here's what actually happens to your strength.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, so every summer I get the same question from athletes and their parents, and it's some version of, if I take a couple weeks off, am I going to lose everything I built? And the honest answer, based on the research, is probably not.

There's a study on adolescent football players, U16 and U19, average age 16, where they completely stopped training for 3 weeks. No lifting, nothing. And when they came back and measured muscle thickness, strength, and sport performance, almost everything held. The only changes were a small bump in fat mass and a small drop in fat-free mass, and those were tiny.

So 3 weeks completely off did not tank their strength or their performance. That's a 16-year-old's body, which recovers and holds onto adaptation really well, but the principle carries up the ladder.

Here's what I tell my athletes. A real break is fine, and sometimes it's exactly what you need, mentally and physically. If you want to hold onto more, you don't need your whole program. One or 2 short sessions a week, a couple of hard sets, keeps the lights on. That's it.

What actually costs you is 3 months of doing nothing, then trying to cram it all back into 2 weeks before camp. Take the break. Come back smart.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Putting your kid in one sport year-round to get ahead is making them more likely to get hurt.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take, and I have the data on this one. The whole model of pick one sport early, specialize, play it year-round so you don't fall behind, that model is hurting kids more than it's helping.

There's a 2026 study that looked at over 2,500 NFL players, guys who actually made it to the highest level. They split them into athletes who played multiple sports in high school and athletes who specialized in just one. The multisport guys were the majority, almost two-thirds of them. And the multisport athletes got injured about 20% less over their careers, played around 12 more games, and lasted almost a full extra season.

These are the guys who made the NFL, and playing more than one sport made them more durable and kept them on the field longer.

I see this all the time with football and lacrosse families. The fear is that if my kid isn't doing the one sport 12 months a year, somebody else is getting ahead. The research keeps saying the opposite. Different sports build different movement patterns and different landing mechanics, and that variety is part of what makes an athlete hard to break.

So let your athlete play the other sport. Let them have an off-season. The kid who's still healthy at 22 wins.
