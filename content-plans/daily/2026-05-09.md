## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** Patellar tendon pain in-season? You can drop it 30 minutes from now without resting.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, here's a clinical move I use almost every week with athletes who can't take time off, but their tendon is screaming.

Patellar tendon pain in-season is the classic problem. The athlete pushes through, the pain gets louder, they start losing power, and the season won't pause for a rest week.

The research here is pretty clear. Heavy isometric holds drop tendon pain in the short term, and the analgesia lasts. A recent systematic review found the pain reduction was sustained at 45 minutes post-exercise. So the athlete warms up with isometrics, walks into practice with a quieter tendon, and actually trains.

The protocol I run is 5 sets of 45-second holds at about 70% of max voluntary contraction. Quiet on a leg extension or a Spanish squat against a band. Aim for tension during the hold. Pain should sit around 2-3/10 the whole time.

The longer-term piece is heavy slow resistance, 3 days a week, slow tempo, working through a rep range that climbs over months. That's where the tendon actually remodels.

In-season, isometrics buy you the day. Off-season, heavy slow resistance builds the tendon. Both have a job.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** If your PT is still prescribing only eccentrics for your patellar tendon, they're working off a 2003 playbook.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take time, and I want to pick a fight with the rehab world for a minute.

Decline-board eccentrics for patellar tendinopathy. That protocol came out in the early 2000s and it's still the first thing a lot of clinicians prescribe. 20 plus years later, the meta-analyses are saying something different.

A 2024 network meta-analysis on patellar tendinopathy compared eccentric, isometric, and heavy slow resistance head to head on the VISA-P score. The line that mattered, and I'm quoting, "the PTLE that includes isometric training or moderate slow resistance is more effective than eccentric training alone."

PTLE means progressive tendon loading exercise. Isometrics for the acute pain. Heavy slow resistance for the long-term adaptation. Eccentrics still have a role, but they shouldn't carry the whole program, and they probably shouldn't be the first tool out of the box.

Here's what bugs me. The decline-squat eccentric is uncomfortable, drives soreness, and most athletes hate it. The field had the option to layer in isometrics for symptom relief and heavier compound lifts for slow remodeling, and a lot of clinics stuck with the old protocol because it was already in the EHR template.

If you've been doing decline eccentrics for 6 weeks and your knee still hurts, the program is the problem.
