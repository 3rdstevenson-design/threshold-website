## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Isometric priming raised these young athletes' force and dropped the velocity end of their profile 24 hours later.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Let's talk about priming day, because I think plenty of programs are running it backwards.

New randomized crossover trial in Sports Health. 10 youth soccer players, 3 days out from a match. One condition was isometric mid thigh pulls, 3 sets of 3 reps, 3 seconds a rep, 10 seconds between reps, 2 minutes between sets. The other condition was their normal training. Retested everybody 24 hours later.

The isometric day did improve theoretical maximal force, and change of direction on the non dominant leg got better, although the authors flag that one as partly a sequencing artifact.

Here's the part I care about. Theoretical maximal velocity went down. Force orientation at high speed got worse. And 30 meter sprint time didn't improve in either group.

It's 10 athletes, so hold the study loosely. The principle I'll state flat. If your athlete's actual gap is that they're slow, a priming day that pushes them toward force and away from velocity is the wrong tool that week. Strength without speed is a paperweight.

Prime for force when force is the gap. The rest of the time, go sprint.

**Caption (copy-paste ready):**
Priming day costs you something, and most programs never pick which direction they're spending in.

New randomized crossover in Sports Health. 10 youth soccer players, 3 days out from a match, did isometric mid thigh pulls at 3 sets of 3 reps of 3 seconds against their normal training, then retested 24 hours later.

What went up: theoretical maximal force, and change of direction on the non dominant leg (the authors flag that one as sequence-influenced, so I'm holding it lightly).

What went down: theoretical maximal velocity, and force orientation at higher speeds. 30 meter sprint time moved in neither condition.

10 athletes is a small study, so I'd treat the numbers as a signal and hold them loosely. The programming logic holds anyway. Priming shifts an athlete along the force velocity profile, and shifting toward force costs you something at the velocity end. If the reason your kid isn't getting on the field is top speed, that's the exact currency you just spent.

Prime for force when force is the measurable gap. The rest of the time, go sprint.
