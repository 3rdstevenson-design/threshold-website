## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** 70% of ACL tears happen when an athlete tries to stop.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, this is the part of athletic development almost no one actually trains. Deceleration.

About 70% of non-contact ACL tears happen in the braking step, the cut, or the hard plant. Almost all of them happen while the athlete is trying to slow down.

When you decelerate, the ground reaction force spikes high and fast, what the researchers call a tall, thin force curve, and your knee, your hip, and your tendons have to absorb all of that in milliseconds.

If you've never trained for that load, the tissue absorbs it poorly. The knee collapses inward, the hip stays high, and the joint takes a hit it was never prepared for.

Here's the fix. Expose the athlete to deceleration on purpose, every week. Short maximal sprints into a controlled stop, on grass, on turf, in the directions they actually play.

In my clinic, when I bring a post-op ACL athlete back to sport, the last clearance test is braking under load, well after the squat and the box jump are cleared.

The CROSS framework calls this teaching the body to absorb force before it produces it.

If you skip it, you're training a faster engine with no brakes.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Being a fit endurance athlete will not save you from menopause.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, here's a conversation I keep having in clinic. Active women in their 40s and 50s assume that because they run, cycle, swim, and lift, menopause is going to glide right past them.

A new PLOS One paper from December says otherwise.

187 female endurance athletes, ages 40 to 60. 88% had sleep problems. 83% reported physical and mental exhaustion. 72% had anxiety. 67% reported weight gain. And 63% had joint and muscular discomfort.

Here's the part that matters for me as a PT. Of the women who had joint and muscle pain, 97% said it hurt their training, and 91% said it hurt their performance.

That joint pain you've been chalking up to your training load probably has a second driver. Estrogen has dropped, and connective tissue handles stress differently when it does.

So here's my hot take. I'm done pretending fitness is a vaccine against menopause. The data is clear.

In my clinic, the women crushing it through perimenopause are the ones lifting hard, sleeping like it's part of training, and getting a real medical workup on their hormones. The ones piling more endurance volume on top of a body in transition show up with tendons that won't recover and joints that ache for weeks.

If you're a coach, ask the question. If you're the athlete, advocate for the workup. Stop trying to out-train it.

Be Good.
