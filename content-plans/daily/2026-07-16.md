## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** Your athlete runs sprints at every practice and still isn't getting faster.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, there's a meta-analysis in PLoS One, 34 randomized trials, 990 adolescent soccer players, and it asked a pretty simple question: if you bolt jumping onto normal practice, do athletes actually get faster?

They do. Sprint times improved at every distance they measured, and the effect was biggest over the first 10 meters, which is the piece that actually shows up in football and lacrosse, because you're rarely running 40 yards clean, you're winning the first two steps.

The mechanism is ground contact. Speed comes from how hard you put force into the floor and how fast you get back off of it, and plyometrics sand that down to short, stiff contacts. Practice makes you tired. Jumping makes you fast.

And the dose is small, probably smaller than you'd think. Twice a week, about 7 weeks, moderate intensity, roughly 1,400 total contacts across the whole block. That's it.

So Monday, before the lift, before practice: 3 sets of 5 pogo hops, 3 sets of 3 broad jumps, full rest between sets, land quiet. Quality every rep. If your athlete is grinding out 200 sloppy jumps at the end of a session when they're already smoked, that's conditioning, and it won't make them faster.

Does that make sense?

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** The athletes who felt the best about their knee are the ones who tore it again.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, this one bothers me, and I want to be careful with how I say it.

204 young athletes, all of them post ACL reconstruction, all of them finished a return to sport bridge program, all of them cleared and released back to their sport around 8 and a half months out. Researchers then followed them for almost 8 years.

17 tore an ACL again, either the graft or the other knee. And when they looked at who those athletes were, the ones who got hurt had scored higher on their knee function questionnaires coming out of the program. They felt better about their knee than everybody else did.

Now, it's an association inside a small number of injuries, so I'm hedging here. But it lines up with what I see in the clinic. Confidence is not a clearance criterion. Feeling ready and being ready are two separate measurements, and only one of them shows up on a test.

So here's my hot take. A lot of return to sport programs are quietly measuring whether the athlete believes they're back. I want strength symmetry side to side, I want hop testing, I want change of direction under fatigue, and I want to watch them move at the end of a session when they're tired and they've stopped thinking about their knee.

The kid who tells me he feels 100% at 8 months? That's the kid I test hardest.
