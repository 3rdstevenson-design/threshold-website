## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Kid pulls a gripper out of his bag between sets, and he's training the one position his grip never fails in.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Kid pulls a gripper out of his bag between sets, and he's training the one position his grip never fails in.

Okay, so a gripper trains a closed crush. Fingers curl toward the palm, the thumb does almost nothing, the wrist stays locked, and the tension peaks right where your hand is already strongest.

Now look at what your grip actually does on the field. A lineman holding a block is an open hand. A lacrosse player keeping his stick through a check is thumb and fingers with no crush at all. A heavy carry is support endurance. A row is a hook that slowly unrolls.

None of that looks like squeezing a spring.

And strength is really specific to the position you train it in. That part is settled, it's about the oldest principle in the field. How much one grip exercise carries over to another is honestly still thin research, so I'll hedge there.

I won't hedge on this. I'd rather my athletes spend those 8 minutes on fat grips for rows and curls, plate pinches, and open hand plate curls, and I'd try chalk before straps, because chalk fixes friction and straps outsource the whole link.

Train the hand positions where you actually lose the bar.

The gripper in your gym bag trains a closed crush. Fingers curl toward the palm, the thumb barely works, the wrist stays locked, and tension peaks exactly where your hand is already strongest.

What I will not hedge on is where I would spend the time: fat grips on rows and curls, plate pinches, open hand plate curls, 8 minutes at the end of a session. And chalk before straps, because chalk fixes a friction problem and straps hand off the whole link.

Train the hand positions where you actually lose the bar.
