## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** 299 pro footballers, 2 full seasons, 67 hamstring strains, and the thing that got them was the week-to-week jump.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, if your athlete just started football or lacrosse preseason, this one matters this week.

New prospective cohort study. 299 elite footballers, GPS units on them across two full seasons, 67 hamstring strains. What separated the injured players from the healthy ones was the size of the week to week jump. Weekly high speed running distance, weekly change in total distance, weekly change in high speed running, all three showed up as significant.

Muscle architecture came along with it. Shorter biceps femoris fascicles and a steeper pennation angle went with higher risk, and longer fascicles went with lower risk.

Here's the honest part. Strength and architecture together explained 12% of the risk, adding running load got it to 20%, which leaves about 80% unexplained. No screen predicts a hamstring strain. Anybody telling you theirs does is guessing.

What I'd do Monday. Build sprint exposure up in steps across preseason instead of jumping straight to full speed on day one, and keep loading the hamstring long and heavy, Nordics and Romanian deadlifts through a big range, all season and not just in July.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** If you're setting your athlete's conditioning off 220 minus their age, you're running the season on a number somebody eyeballed in 1970.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. 220 minus your age needs to leave the weight room.

New study, 4,375 endurance athletes, their real max heart rate from actual training and racing compared against the age formulas. The formulas came in low by about 5 to 6 beats on average, and the limits of agreement ran from 20 beats below to 9 beats above. That's a 29 beat window on the one number every zone underneath it is built from.

With young athletes it runs the other way. In 147 team sport kids across soccer, futsal, basketball and water polo, 220 minus age came in higher than their measured max. So you're chasing a 15 year old toward a ceiling they don't actually have, and calling it conditioning.

The authors' words: age based formulas should be abandoned as primary tools.

So measure it. Warm up 5 to 8 minutes, run a hard progressive test in the mode they actually train in, chest strap on, and take the highest number you see. That's their max until something better shows up.

Does that make sense?
