## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** If your athlete is dropping weight in August camp, muscle is the first thing to go.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, it's the first week of August, which means two a days, and a whole lot of athletes are quietly eating way less than they're burning.

Here's what happens when you're in that hole. Researchers at Arkansas and the Army put 19 trained young men through 5 day energy deficits at about 30 percent below what they actually needed, kept dietary protein high the entire time, and then traced where the amino acids went. Muscle is last in line. Your liver, your immune system, your gut lining, your connective tissue, they all get paid first, and muscle gets a thank you note.

The dosing piece is the part I'd actually change on Monday. About 0.25 to 0.30 grams of protein per kilogram per feeding maxes out the muscle building signal, and when they tripled that dose, muscle protein synthesis did not budge. It did improve whole body protein balance, which is recovery, immune function, connective tissue repair, kind of everything that isn't your biceps.

So the fix is boring. Eat enough total food during camp. Then put 25 to 35 grams of protein at 4 points in the day, and make one of them right after practice.

You can't supplement your way out of a calorie hole. Does that make sense?

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Zone 2 is the most overrated thing in high school conditioning right now.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. Zone 2 got sold to your athletes by longevity podcasts, and now I've got football and lacrosse kids jogging 45 minutes at conversational pace in August and calling it building a base.

A 2025 narrative review in Sports Medicine went at this directly. The authors asked whether Zone 2 really is the best intensity for mitochondrial and fat oxidation capacity, and they concluded the current evidence doesn't support it, and that prioritizing intensities above Zone 2 matters more, especially when your training volume is low.

Your athlete's conditioning volume is low. They've got school, practice, lifting, and a game on Friday. A professional endurance athlete has 25 hours a week to spend on this. Your kid has about 3.

And in youth athletes specifically, a 2025 meta analysis found high intensity intervals beat general training for VO2 max, with less total training time on the clock.

Here's what I'd rather see. 2 conditioning days a week, short repeats at the speeds the sport actually asks for, full recovery in between, and let the practice sessions carry the easy aerobic work.

The base gets built by playing the sport, right?

## Runner-up topics (next time)
- EAAs and cognitive performance under acute stress (Ferrando, ISSN 2026 conference pilot): reaction time held near baseline with EAAs while placebo slipped roughly 28 ms. Unpublished, 8 subjects, blood draws on 5. Worth revisiting only if it gets published, and the adolescent-athlete "brain under fatigue late in a game" angle is strong.
- Adding L-tyrosine on top of a complete EAA blend gave no advantage over EAAs alone in the same pilot. Good ammo against stacked "focus" formulas marketed to athletes, once published.
- HIIT vs sport-specific drills for repeated sprint ability in male team-sport athletes (PLOS One meta-analysis): no difference for RSA, which is a useful counterweight to today's Draft B and would make a fair, self-critiquing follow-up.
