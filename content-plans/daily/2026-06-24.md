## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** Most ACL tears happen the moment you plant and slow down to cut.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, here's the thing about blowing out a knee. The big force lands when you decelerate, when you plant that foot to cut or stop and your muscles have to soak up all of it at once. That soaking up is eccentric work, the muscle lengthening under load, and if your brakes are weak the ligament ends up taking the hit.

So researchers took professional team sport athletes in late-stage ACL recovery and split them into two groups, one doing traditional strength work and one doing eccentric-oriented strength work, 2 to 3 sessions a week for 6 weeks.

The eccentric group won across the board. Bigger gains in leg strength, in vertical jump, and in the single-leg and triple hop tests on the surgical leg, with effect sizes they called large to very large.

Here's what I do with my football and lacrosse guys. We train the lowering. Slow the descent on squats and split squats, 3 to 4 seconds down. We add real deceleration drills, sprint and stick the stop, cut and hold the landing. And we hammer single-leg strength, because the field never asks you to land on two feet.

You don't have to be hurt to train this. Strong brakes are how you stay on the field in the first place.

And honestly this carries over to active adults. Walking down stairs, stepping off a curb, that's all your brakes working. Build them and the knee complains a lot less.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** "Never static stretch before you play, it kills your power" is a myth coaches have repeated for 20 years.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. The whole "never static stretch before you compete, it kills your power" rule is mostly wrong, and coaches have been repeating it for 20 years.

Here's where it came from. Studies showed that if you hold a long static stretch and then immediately test maximal strength by itself, force drops. Real effect, and a big one once the stretch goes past 60 seconds.

But here's the part that matters for an athlete. A big meta-analysis just revisited all of it, and when they looked at actual performance, jumping, sprinting, change of direction, that deficit basically disappeared. The authors flat out said their results do not support the old advice to pull static stretching out of warm-ups before jumping or sprinting.

So what do I tell my guys. Don't hang in a 90-second hamstring stretch right before kickoff, that's still not how I'd warm up. But a few short stretches on a tight spot, 20 or 30 seconds, inside a warm-up that also has movement and build-up sprints, that's totally fine. It won't cost you a step.

A good warm-up is what protects your first hard cut. Get the heart rate up, move through the ranges you're about to use, hit a couple of build-up sprints, and yeah, stretch the cranky spot if you want to.

Does that make sense? The science moved on. A lot of sidelines haven't.
