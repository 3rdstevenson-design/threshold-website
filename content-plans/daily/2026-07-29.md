## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** If you've torn a hamstring before, that hamstring can still change just as much as everybody else's.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, if you've torn a hamstring, somebody has probably told you that leg is different now, that it won't respond the way it used to, and that you're going to be managing it forever.

A new study out of Dublin says something else. 26 elite under-20 Gaelic football players, 9 weeks into their competitive season, one lifting session a week. 2 sets of 4 Nordic hamstring curls, and 3 sets of 6 staggered stance Romanian deadlifts. That's the whole hamstring program.

After 9 weeks the long head of the biceps femoris got longer, about 0.31 centimeters of fascicle length on average. And the limbs that had already been injured in the last year changed just as much as the limbs that had never been hurt.

The honest part is that eccentric strength didn't move. So once a week is probably enough to change the architecture of the muscle, and it's probably not enough to build strength. If strength is the goal, you need more than that.

So Monday: 2 sets of 4 Nordics, 3 sets of 6 staggered stance RDLs, once a week, all season, and keep sprinting, because these guys were running full speed in training and matches the whole time.

Your old hamstring injury doesn't lock you out of adapting.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** The preseason movement screen score is the most useless number in a high school athletic program.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take. That preseason movement screen score is the most useless number in a high school athletic program.

New prospective study, June 2026, university basketball players, followed for a year. They ran the standard 7-part screen, they used the classic cutoff of 14 or below, and then they waited to see who actually got hurt. Sensitivity of 0.38, specificity of 0.61, area under the curve of 0.52. That's a coin flip.

And in high school football, lacrosse, and baseball players, an earlier study found the same thing, where neither the total score nor the individual pieces flagged who was going to get injured.

Here's the part I'll defend. The individual patterns did carry signal in this study, the deep squat and the hurdle step specifically, and when the researchers weighted the items instead of just adding them up, the prediction got better. So the movements are worth watching, and the single number on the clipboard is worth kind of ignoring.

What actually moves injury risk in my athletes is training history, previous injury, how much high speed running they've banked, and how strong they are. Screen if you like screening. Just don't hand an athlete a 15 and call them safe.

**Caption (copy-paste ready):**
The preseason movement screen score is close to useless for predicting injury.

June 2026 prospective cohort, university basketball players followed for a year. The classic total-score cutoff of 14 or below produced a sensitivity of 0.38, a specificity of 0.61, and an area under the curve of 0.52. That is a coin flip.

An earlier study in high school football, lacrosse, and baseball players found neither the composite score nor the individual task scores predicted lower extremity injury.

The nuance worth keeping: individual patterns did carry signal here (deep squat and hurdle step), and weighting the items instead of summing them lifted prediction to an AUC of 0.75. Watch the movement. Stop trusting the total.

What actually moves risk: training history, previous injury, accumulated high speed running, and strength.

It's time to cross your threshold.
