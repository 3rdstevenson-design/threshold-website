## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel

**Hook:** Your ACL tear might start at your foot.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, so there's a new systematic review out of the University of Kansas, 48 studies on noncontact ACL injuries, and they went looking somewhere the knee research usually skips. The foot.

Here's what they found. Athletes who landed on their forefoot were protected. Athletes who landed flat footed or heel first, or with their toes turned in or turned out, kept showing up in the higher risk group. Same story with medial arch collapse, that navicular drop where the arch flattens under load, and with greater side to side rearfoot motion.

Prior ankle injury was in there too, which tracks with what I see. An athlete rolls an ankle in September, it gets iced for a week, they're back at practice, and now they're cutting off a foot that can't organize itself, and the knee ends up eating that.

So what do you do Monday? Watch the foot on landings. Film a drop jump from the front and look at two things, whether the arch dumps and where the toes point when they hit. If the arch collapses or the foot spins, that's your first target, and it's a lot easier to train than a reconstructed knee.

The review is honest that the study quality here is mostly low, so this is a place to look, probably not a rule yet.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel

**Hook:** The beet shot before your game is doing nothing for your speed.

**Spoken script (word for word):**

Okay, hot take. If your athlete is drinking a beet shot before a game to run faster, save the money.

New study in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. 12 trained male sprinters, randomized, double blind, placebo controlled. They got 70 milliliters of beetroot juice or a placebo, then they jumped, and then they ran a 60 and a 100.

The juice absolutely worked at the chemistry level. Salivary nitrate went up more than 20 fold. So it got in, it was absorbed, the dose was real.

And then nothing moved. Countermovement jump, same. Squat jump, same. 60 meter, same. 100 meter, same. They even measured contractile properties of the quad, the hamstring, and both calves with tensiomyography, and found basically nothing there either.

Here's my read. Nitrate has decent evidence in longer efforts where oxygen cost is the limiter. A 100 meter is over before that mechanism ever becomes the thing holding you back. So the athlete drinking beets to get faster is buying a solution to a problem they don't have.

It's 12 guys and one acute dose, so hold it loosely. But if you want to get faster, sprint at full speed every week, sleep 9 hours, and get strong. That's the stack, and it's free.
