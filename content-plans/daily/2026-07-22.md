## Draft A — Research-grounded Reel
**Hook:** The hamstring you pull in October gets decided in August.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, camp starts in a couple of weeks, so let's talk about the hamstring you pull in October.

Researchers put GPS units on 67 elite soccer players and tracked them across 3 seasons. They logged the whole preseason: total distance, high-speed distance, sprint distance, sprint count, accelerations, decelerations. Then they waited and counted who strained a hamstring once the season started.

The variable that showed up was sprint distance. More preseason sprint distance meant lower in-season hamstring injury odds, roughly 10% lower per unit, and sprint count was even stronger than that.

And that tracks with how the injury actually happens, right? A hamstring strains at the end of the swing phase, when it's lengthening fast under high velocity. Only real sprinting puts the tissue in that position at that speed. Reps at true top speed are the rehearsal.

So going into camp, get max velocity work on the calendar. 2 or 3 days a week, 20 to 40 yards, walk all the way back, full recovery between reps. If you're breathing hard, you're conditioning, and that's a different session on a different day.

This was pro soccer players, so I'd hold it a little loosely for a high school lineman. The mechanism carries over to football and lacrosse though, and the fix is free.

Sprinting is what makes you durable at sprinting.

## Draft B — Hot take Reel
**Hook:** Every August a camp sells you neck training as concussion insurance. Two reviews say it isn't.

**Spoken script (word for word):**
Okay, hot take for August. Neck training probably doesn't prevent concussions, and I program it anyway.

Here's where the belief came from. A study years back reported that for every pound of neck strength, concussion odds dropped about 5%. That one number went everywhere. It's on the flyer for your kid's camp, it's in every neck trainer ad, it's the whole pitch.

Then people pooled the research. A meta-analysis in JOSPT gathered 8 studies and 7,625 athletes and found a small, nonsignificant relationship, graded very low certainty, with heterogeneity over 90%, which kind of means the studies wildly disagreed with each other. And a new systematic review this January in Current Sports Medicine Reports went back through it and landed on inconclusive.

So why is neck work still on my programs? Because it earns its spot on things I can actually see. Stingers. Cervical strain. Head and eye position going into contact. Holding a good tackling posture in the 4th quarter.

That's a real sell, and it's honest.

When you market neck work as a concussion shield and a kid gets concussed anyway, that family stops believing anything you say about prevention. You just spent trust you needed later.

Train the neck. Sell it on what it actually does.
